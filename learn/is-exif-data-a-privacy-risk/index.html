Is EXIF data a privacy risk?

Sometimes. The risk comes from GPS and date tags in the original file, not from the picture itself.

Updated 2026-11-19

Direct answer: EXIF is a privacy risk when you share the original file of a photo that contains GPS coordinates. Anyone who receives that file can read where and when it was taken. It is low risk when the platform strips it, or when Location Services was off at capture.

A documented example

In December 2012, a photo of software founder John McAfee published by a magazine while he was a fugitive carried GPS tags in its EXIF data. Readers extracted the coordinates and located him in Guatemala, and the magazine removed the photo. It is the standard example of a geotag giving away a location the subject was hiding.

Simple routine

  1. Check: does the photo have location.
  2. Remove location or all metadata: on iPhone or in the browser.
  3. Share the cleaned copy, not the original.

FAQ

Can someone find my home from a photo?

If the original file has GPS tags and you share it unchanged, yes. Remove location before sharing photos taken at home.

Do social networks show my EXIF to others?

Major platforms generally strip it from the public copy, but behaviour varies and the original may be stored, so do not rely on it.

Is it enough to turn off Location Services?

It stops new GPS tags. Photos already taken keep theirs until you remove them.

Does EXIF contain my name?

Only if a camera or app wrote Artist or Copyright fields. Most phones do not.
